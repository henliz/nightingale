HOW NIGHTINGALE WORKS
a plain-english guide for people who don't write code
(written oct 20 2026, describes the system as it is today)

================================================================
FIRST, THE NAMES
================================================================

"Nightingale" is two things, so here's how to keep them straight:

  - NIGHTINGALE THE DEVICE. A tiny computer called a Raspberry Pi
    (the model is a "Pi Zero 2 W", about the size of a stick of gum).
    We named this particular Pi "nightingale". When anything on the
    network talks to it, that's the name it answers to. It has a
    camera, a microphone and two buttons, and it's meant to be worn
    by a first responder.

  - NIGHTINGALE THE SYSTEM. The whole project: the device, plus the
    server that receives what the device records, plus the AI that
    turns the recording into a draft patient report, plus the
    dashboard where you watch it all happen.

When this guide says "the Pi" or "the device", it means the little
computer named nightingale. When it says "the server" or "the
laptop", it means the computer receiving the recordings (right now
that's Hen's MacBook).


================================================================
THE BIG PICTURE IN ONE PARAGRAPH
================================================================

A firefighter presses a button on the Pi. It turns on and starts
recording what's being said at the scene. Whenever they press the
other button, it takes a photo. The recording is cut into short
15-second pieces, and each piece is sent over Wi-Fi to the server
as soon as it's ready. The server turns the speech into text, works
out who is speaking, and fills in a draft ePCR (electronic patient
care report: the form paramedics fill out for every patient). It
updates that draft every time a new piece arrives, so the form fills
itself in while the call is still happening. When the firefighter
presses the first button again, the Pi saves everything and turns
itself off. Nothing is ever deleted from the Pi until the server has
confirmed it has a perfect copy.


================================================================
PART 1: THE DEVICE (the Pi named nightingale)
================================================================

WHAT'S PHYSICALLY ON IT

  - The Pi itself (the small computer).
  - A camera with autofocus (Arducam 16MP), so photos are sharp
    even though the person wearing it is moving.
  - A microphone. Right now it's the Pi's basic input, which is very
    quiet. A better USB sound card is coming.
  - Two buttons.
  - A memory card (like a phone's storage), which is where
    recordings wait until they've been uploaded.
  - Battery or power bank power.

THE TWO BUTTONS (this is the whole interface, on purpose)

  1. POWER / RECORD
     - If the device is off: pressing it turns the Pi on. As soon as
       it has started up (it takes a little while to boot), it starts
       recording automatically. You don't have to press anything else.
     - If it's recording: pressing it stops the recording, saves it
       safely, and turns the Pi off.
     - Safety net: if someone forgets it's on, it stops by itself
       after 30 minutes and does the same thing.

  2. PHOTO
     - While it's recording, a press takes one photo.
     - While it's not recording, the button does nothing.

One press always means one action. There are no "hold for 3
seconds" tricks. The device NEVER takes photos by itself, and it
never records unless someone pressed the button to turn it on.

When recording starts, the device reminds the responder to say out
loud: "I'm recording for the medical record", so everyone at the
scene knows.

HOW IT RECORDS AUDIO

  - It records in good-quality, uncompressed sound (the format is
    called WAV). That's plain enough for speech-to-text to work
    well, and it's never squashed in a way that loses detail.
  - The recording is quietly split into 15-second pieces ("chunks")
    one after another, with no gap between them. The person wearing
    it never notices. This is so pieces can be sent off and turned
    into text while the call is still going, instead of waiting
    until the end.
  - At the same time, it also sends a "live" feed of the microphone
    straight to the server, so words can appear on the dashboard
    almost as they're spoken. The live feed is a bonus. The saved
    15-second pieces are the real record. If the live feed drops,
    nothing is lost.

HOW IT TAKES A PHOTO

  - On a PHOTO press, the camera takes a quick burst of several
    frames, not just one.
  - The Pi scores each frame for sharpness (basically, "how crisp
    are the edges in this picture?"), keeps only the sharpest one,
    and immediately deletes the rest. So a shaky hand doesn't ruin
    the shot, and no extra images are kept.
  - Autofocus happens at the moment of capture.

THE WAITING ROOM (the "queue") AND WHY NOTHING GETS LOST

This is the most important safety idea in the device.

  1. While a piece of audio or a photo is being written, it lives in
     a holding folder called "incoming". That way a half-finished
     file is never sent.
  2. Once it's finished, it moves into the queue: a line of files
     waiting to be sent.
  3. Separate background helpers on the Pi keep taking the oldest
     file in the line and sending it to the server.
  4. The file is only deleted from the Pi once the server replies
     "got it, and it's a perfect copy". (How does it know it's
     perfect? See "fingerprints" below.)
  5. If the Wi-Fi drops, the files just wait in line. If the battery
     dies mid-recording, whatever was half-written is rescued the
     next time the Pi turns on, and uploaded then.
  6. Turning the Pi off never waits for the internet. If you press
     stop with no Wi-Fi, everything stays in the queue and uploads
     the next time the device is on and connected.

This pattern has a name: STORE-AND-FORWARD. Store it safely first,
forward it when you can.

FINGERPRINTS (sha256)

Before sending a file, the Pi calculates a "fingerprint" of it: a
long code that's different for every possible file. Change a single
bit and the fingerprint is completely different. The server
calculates the fingerprint of what it received. If the two match,
the copy is perfect. If not, the Pi keeps its copy and tries again.

WHAT THE PI DOES NOT DO

  - No AI, no speech-to-text, no thinking on the device. It just
    records, takes photos and sends them. All the smart work happens
    on the server. (The Pi is small and has very little memory.)
  - No video. Photos and audio only. That was a deliberate decision.
  - It never listens to anything except its own microphone. If a
    transcript ever looks like radio chatter or something nobody
    said, that's the speech-to-text AI making things up from quiet
    audio, not the device picking up outside signals.

HOW THE PI KNOWS WHAT IT'S DOING (logs)

The Pi keeps a running diary of what it's doing ("started
recording", "photo saved", "uploaded piece 4", "Wi-Fi lost"). During
drills it also sends that diary to the server so it shows up live
on the dashboard. Every 30 seconds it sends a "heartbeat": I'm on,
here's how many files are waiting, how full my storage is, how hot
I am. The diary NEVER contains what was said or any images. It's
only "what happened", never "what was recorded".


================================================================
PART 2: HOW THE PI TALKS TO THE SERVER (today)
================================================================

THE CONNECTION

  - Today, the Pi uses Wi-Fi. It remembers networks it has joined
    before (home Wi-Fi, or Hen's phone hotspot) and connects to
    whichever one it can see.
  - The server is Hen's laptop, and the two have to be on the SAME
    Wi-Fi network to find each other. The Pi finds the laptop by
    name (Henriettas-MacBook-Air.local), so nobody has to type
    addresses.
  - This is fine for drills, but it means the laptop has to be
    nearby, awake, and on the same network. That's the main
    limitation of today's setup (see Part 5).

WHAT ACTUALLY GETS SENT, AND HOW

Everything travels the same way any website does: over the internet
standard called HTTP. Behind the scenes, the data is chopped into
tiny "packets" that travel separately and get reassembled at the
other end. That's true of everything on the internet, including
this.

For each file (an audio piece or a photo), the Pi does three steps:

  1. "I'm about to send you a file. Here's what kind it is and its
     fingerprint." The server replies with a ticket number.
  2. It sends the file itself (in parts if it's big, though in
     practice our files fit in one part).
  3. "I'm done." The server puts the parts together, checks the
     fingerprint, and replies "verified" or "no, try again".

If the connection drops halfway, the Pi can ask "where did I get
to?" and pick up from there instead of starting over.

And separately, the whole time it's recording:

  - the live microphone feed (for words appearing on screen as
    they're spoken),
  - the diary and heartbeat (for the dashboard's console).


================================================================
PART 3: WHAT THE SERVER DOES WITH IT
================================================================

STEP 1: KEEP IT SAFE FIRST

Before the server says "verified" to the Pi, it writes the exact
bytes it received into a protected folder on the laptop called the
CRASH PAD. That folder is read-only, sits outside the project, and
keeps its own list of fingerprints. The Pi deletes its copy as soon
as it hears "verified", so the crash pad is what guarantees there's
always one untouched original somewhere. If the crash pad can't save
the file, the server tells the Pi "no", and the Pi keeps its copy.

The file also goes into the VAULT, a secure storage service running
on the laptop. Everything after this point (text, the report, the
dashboard) works on copies. Any of those steps can fail without
losing the recording.

STEP 2: SKIP SILENCE

If a 15-second piece is basically silent, it isn't sent to the AI at
all. Speech-to-text AIs tend to invent words out of silence, and we
don't want made-up sentences ending up in a patient report.

STEP 3: SPEECH TO TEXT, WITH WHO SAID WHAT

Each piece is sent to Google's Gemini speech-to-text AI. It returns
the words, labelled by voice: "Person 1", "Person 2", and so on.
Those are numbered in the order the voices were first heard, NOT by
role. The AI doesn't know who the firefighter is.

To keep "Person 2" as the same human from one piece to the next, the
server plays a short sample of each voice it has already heard in
front of the new piece. That way the AI can match voices up.

STEP 4: FILL IN THE DRAFT ePCR

Every time new text arrives, the server hands the WHOLE transcript
so far to a second AI. That AI fills in the patient report the way a
paramedic would chart it:

  - incident: where, why the call was made, who's on scene, and the
    times (call placed, crew arrived, patient contact, handoff,
    departure)
  - scene: number of patients, environment, hazards
  - patient: name, age, sex, whether they can make their own
    decisions ("capacity") and who decides for them if not
  - chief complaint and what happened
  - SAMPLE history and OPQRST (standard paramedic checklists about
    symptoms, allergies, medications, and what the pain is like)
  - vitals (blood pressure, pulse, and so on, but only numbers
    someone actually said out loud)
  - interventions (what the responders did, and when)
  - handoff to paramedics, and a short written summary

The rules it follows are strict:

  - It can restate and combine what people said in proper medical
    wording.
  - It can NOT add facts nobody said: no diagnosis, no guessed
    numbers, no guessed left or right side, no guessed names or
    ages.
  - If two people disagree (one says 32 years old, another says
    57), it records both and flags it.
  - Anything it's unsure about goes in a "needs review" list.

STEP 5: PHOTOS

Each photo gets a one-sentence description of only what's visible
(for example "a person lying on a mat, attended by two others").
No guessing, no diagnosis.

STEP 6: THE DASHBOARD

Open http://localhost:8787 on the laptop to see three panels:

  - the draft ePCR, filling itself in, with changed fields briefly
    lighting up
  - the transcript, with who said what and when
  - the console: the Pi's diary, live

The dashboard updates the moment new data arrives. It doesn't
wait to refresh.

Every session is also saved to a "drills" folder on the laptop
(audio, photos, transcript, report) and comes back if the server
restarts.

IMPORTANT: EVERYTHING IS A DRAFT

Nothing the AI writes is final. Every field is meant to be reviewed
and approved by the responder before it goes anywhere. The system
summarizes what was said. It does not diagnose, and it never gives
treatment advice.


================================================================
PART 4: ONE CALL, START TO FINISH
================================================================

  1. A firefighter presses POWER/RECORD. Nightingale starts up and
     begins recording. The responder says "I'm recording for the
     medical record".
  2. They talk to the patient and bystanders. Every 15 seconds a
     piece of audio joins the queue and is sent to the laptop.
  3. They press PHOTO once at the scene. Nightingale takes a burst,
     keeps the sharpest frame, and queues it.
  4. On the laptop, each piece is fingerprint-checked, copied to the
     crash pad and the vault, turned into labelled text, and the
     draft ePCR is updated. The dashboard shows it all as it lands.
  5. Paramedics arrive. The firefighter gives the handoff report out
     loud, and the ePCR records "transfer of care to paramedics".
  6. The firefighter presses POWER/RECORD again. Nightingale saves
     the last piece and turns off. Anything that hadn't uploaded yet
     goes up the next time it's on and connected.
  7. The finished draft is waiting on the dashboard for review.


================================================================
PART 5: WHAT'S NEXT, MOVING OFF THE LAPTOP
================================================================

THE PROBLEM WITH TODAY'S SETUP

The server is Hen's laptop. The Pi can only send things when the
laptop is on, awake, and on the same Wi-Fi. If the laptop closes, or
the folder on it gets messed up, recordings are at risk. Real first
responders can't depend on someone's laptop, so the server has to
move to the cloud: computers that are always on and reachable from
anywhere.

The plan is Vercel, a cloud hosting service. It happens in two
steps.

STEP A: CLOUD SERVER, PI STILL ON WI-FI (next)

  - The server moves to a web address (something like
    https://nightingale.vercel.app) instead of the laptop.
  - The Pi still connects over Wi-Fi, but ANY Wi-Fi with internet
    now works: home, the venue, or a phone hotspot. The laptop
    doesn't have to be there at all.
  - No new hardware is needed. The Pi already has Wi-Fi. For a phone
    hotspot on an iPhone, turn on "Maximize Compatibility", because
    this Pi only speaks the older 2.4 GHz kind of Wi-Fi.
  - Behind the scenes, a few things change to fit how the cloud
    works:
      - Files go straight from the Pi into cloud storage (Vercel
        Blob or similar) through a one-time, short-lived upload
        link. The cloud's storage takes over the crash pad's job of
        being the safe original.
      - Sessions and reports live in a proper database instead of a
        folder on the laptop.
      - Speech-to-text and the report run as background jobs,
        triggered each time a new piece arrives.
      - The dashboard gets its live updates through a "realtime"
        service.
  - The safety rule stays the same: the Pi only deletes a file once
    the cloud confirms a perfect copy (matching fingerprint).
  - Because this is patient information, it also needs: a login so
    only the right people can see anything, encryption, and data
    stored in Canada (Ontario's health privacy law, PHIPA).

STEP B: PROPERLY WIRELESS (later)

  - The goal is a device that works anywhere, with no Wi-Fi network
    to join.
  - Option 1, tether to a phone: the Pi connects to the responder's
    phone (over Wi-Fi hotspot, or Bluetooth), and the phone's
    cellular data carries everything to the cloud. No extra
    hardware.
  - Option 2, its own cellular connection: add a small cellular
    modem (a USB stick or an add-on board, for example a SIM7600)
    and a SIM card with a data plan, like a phone's. It works
    anywhere there's cell signal. The cost is a bit of extra size
    and more battery drain.
  - Either way, store-and-forward means a weak or patchy signal is
    fine. The files wait in the queue and catch up when the signal
    comes back.

QUICK GLOSSARY
================================================================

  Raspberry Pi    a tiny, cheap computer. Ours is named
                  "nightingale".
  ePCR            electronic patient care report, the official form
                  for each patient.
  chunk / piece   a 15-second slice of the recording.
  queue           the line of files on the Pi waiting to be sent.
  store-and-      save it safely first, send it when there's a
  forward         connection.
  sha256 /        a unique code for a file, used to prove a copy
  fingerprint     is perfect.
  crash pad       today's protected, read-only folder on the laptop
                  that keeps the exact original of everything
                  received.
  vault           the secure storage where originals are kept.
  HTTP / packets  how all data moves over the internet: in small
                  pieces, reassembled at the other end.
  cloud /         computers run by a company (Vercel) that are
  Vercel          always on and reachable from anywhere.
  tethering       a device using a phone's internet connection.
  diarization     the AI working out which voice is speaking.
